# Turn the Dial: Game Concept

Oct 15, 2026 · @Nathan · Consumed Media · Working title

## The pitch

**A roguelike deckbuilder where your deck is the broadcast.** You run a scrappy local radio station, and every card is something you put on air: songs, DJ breaks, ads, contests, station IDs. Program the right hour and listeners turn the dial to you. Program the wrong one and they turn it to the other guy.

**Player fantasy.** Minute one: you are a program director with a cheap board, three crates of records, and a rival across town eating your lunch. Hour five: you are stringing tempo ramps into a power hit while your rival cuts to a six-minute stopset, and you can watch the whole town light up on your frequency.

**Why it can win.** The best recent deckbuilders all turn a familiar thing into the deck (poker, coin pushers, claw machines). Radio is familiar to everyone, nobody has made this game, and Consumed Media knows the industry from the inside. That authenticity is the marketing angle.

## Core rules

Every fight is a **daypart** against a rival station: morning drive, midday, afternoon drive, overnight. It plays like a classic deckbuilder fight, re-skinned so every number means something in radio.

| Radio term | Genre equivalent | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Listeners | Health | Your audience. Carries across the whole run. Hit zero and you are off the air. |
| Rival audience | Enemy health | Pull it to zero and you win the daypart. |
| Reach | Attack | Pulls listeners away from the rival. |
| Loyalty | Block | Protects your audience from the rival's next move. Clears each turn. |
| Airtime | Energy | Minutes in the hour. Spend it to play cards. 3 per turn to start. |
| Cash | Gold | Earned from ads and wins. Spent at sponsor meetings. |

**The rival's next move is always visible.** Above the rival's frequency you see what they are about to air: a star segment, a contest, a commercial break. You program around it. A rival cutting to commercials is your opening to steal listeners. This is how real stations compete, and it answers the genre's biggest complaint: randomness you can't see or play around.

**Dead Air.** If you end a turn without putting a song or talk segment on air, you take a Dead Air penalty and lose listeners. It is the game's signature rule: never let the hour go silent.

## Cards and combos

| Card type | Role | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Songs | The backbone. Each has a genre and a tempo (1 to 5). Main source of Reach. | Power Ballad: 2 Airtime, 9 Reach. Tempo 2. |
| DJ breaks | Cheap setup cards that boost the next song or build Loyalty. | Back-Sell: next song gets +50% Reach. |
| Ads | Earn Cash now, cost listeners or Loyalty. The core tension of radio. | Car Dealer Spot: +25 Cash, lose 4 listeners. |
| Promos and contests | Build Hype that pays off over the next few turns. | Caller Nine Wins: gain 3 Hype. |
| Liners and jingles | Zero or one Airtime glue cards that link combos. | Station ID: keep your Flow going. |
| Interference | Junk cards the rival slips into your deck, like static or a complaint call. | Static: unplayable, clogs your hand. |

**Flow** is the combo system. Sequencing songs builds Flow stacks that multiply Reach:

- **Tempo ramp:** play songs in rising tempo for a growing multiplier.
- **Genre streak:** consecutive songs in your format's genre add bonus Reach.
- **The drop:** DJ breaks and power hits cash in Flow for a big burst.
- **Breaking Flow:** playing an ad mid-streak drops your stacks. Do you take the money?

**Status effects:** Hype (boosts Reach), Buzz (a rival show is trending, take more steal), Burnout (a song played too often loses Reach), Static (Interference cards in your deck).

## Run structure

One run is **one ratings season**, split into three **books** (acts). Each book is a branching route through town, shown as an isometric diorama map where every node is a real place, ending in a ratings book boss. Target run length: 35 to 45 minutes, with save and quit at any node.

| Node | Genre equivalent | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daypart | Fight | A rival show tries to take your audience. |
| Prime Time | Elite | A tougher rival segment with a better reward. |
| Sponsor Meeting | Shop | Buy cards and gear, or pay to cut a card from your playlist. |
| Production Room | Rest site | Recover listeners, or remaster (upgrade) a card. |
| Station Event | Mystery node | Short text events: a van remote, a contest gone wrong, a call from the GM. |
| Remote Broadcast | Treasure | Pick up a piece of gear. |
| Ratings Book | Boss | The act-ending showdown. |

**Rivals** are shows and stations with personalities: the chaotic Morning Zoo, Smooth Jazz 98.3 that quietly heals, the Sports Talk Guys who hit hard and burn out, a Playlist Bot that copies your genre.

**Bosses** (one per book in v1): a syndicated national morning show, a streaming giant whose algorithm adapts to your format, and a corporate format flip that rewrites the rules mid-fight.

**Gear and staff** are the relics. They sit on your board and change how the run plays: a vintage ribbon mic, a 50,000-watt transmitter, a traffic helicopter, a prize closet. Staff carry personality traits like Old Reliable (steady Loyalty every turn) and Loose Cannon (big visible swings).

## Formats, markets, and progression

**Formats are the starter decks**, the genre's version of characters. Each one plays differently:

| Format | Playstyle |
| --- | --- |
| Classic Rock | Steady and durable. Big single hits, strong Loyalty, forgiving for new players. |
| Hip-Hop Heat | Combo engine. Long tempo ramps and Flow chains, high risk, high ceiling. |
| Country Roads | Community and sustain. Listener mail, call-ins, and slow-building Hype. |
| Talk Radio (post-launch) | No songs at all. Segments, callers, and arguments. A true twist format. |

**Markets are the difficulty ladder.** Each market adds modifiers on top of the last, like the genre's ascension levels. The city types come from the Drive Time Tycoon design: Chill Coastal Village (tutorial), College Town, Blue-Collar Heartland, Retiree Coast, True Crime Suburbia, Big Metro.

**Progression adds options, not power.** Playing runs unlocks new cards, gear, and events into the pool, plus new formats and markets. A first-time player can win with what they start with. No grinding, no permanent stat upgrades. This answers the genre's second biggest complaint.

## Design pillars

Each pillar answers a complaint that came up again and again in reviews of Balatro, Slay the Spire, Monster Train, and other genre games. When a design decision is unclear, these settle it.

1. **Randomness you can see and play around.** Rival moves are always shown. Map nodes are labeled. Tools to bend luck: rerolls, peeks, holding a card.
2. **Every boss has an answer.** Boss rules are telegraphed, and every one can be countered by a deck you could reasonably build.
3. **Unlocks add options, not power.** Fun from the first run, no grind walls.
4. **Familiar structure, new core.** Keep the map, shops, events, and relics players expect. The radio broadcast is what's new.
5. **Readable everywhere.** Big card text, a tooltip on every keyword, a speed toggle, full controller support, Steam Deck verified at launch.
6. **Broken combos stay broken.** Balance by adding counters and content, not by nerfing what players love after launch.
7. **8+ hours before you've seen it all.** All content is data, so it's cheap to add.

## Look and sound

**Art direction: in the spirit of Kind Words 2.** Chunky, rounded, simplified low-poly shapes with soft bevels and slightly exaggerated proportions. Flat colors with gentle gradients and a few hand-painted accents (leaf clusters, chalk doodles, painted signage). Soft lighting, soft shadows, a little bloom on anything that glows. Cozy, calm, slightly dreamy. We take the principles, not the elements: every model, icon, and panel is our own design.

**Two kinds of view (option A).**

- **Fights: the desk view.** A fixed seat in the booth, Strange Horticulture style, so every readout is large enough for a Steam Deck. The booth's objects are the HUD.
- **Everything else: dioramas.** The town map, title screen, shops, and events are high three-quarter isometric scenes, like little models on a table. The station sits on its own patch of ground and the map's nodes are real places in town: the diner, the record store, the county fair. This is the look the trailer and capsule are built on.

**One color wash per daypart.** Like Kind Words 2's one-color scenes, each daypart bathes the whole booth in its own light, so time moving through the season is something you feel:

| Daypart | Scene color |
| --- | --- |
| Morning drive | Pale, cool pink and gold. Hazy sky, low sun on the left, soft gentle light |
| Midday | Pale sky blue and lavender |
| Afternoon drive | Deep, warm orange and coral. Low sun on the right, long strong shadows across the desk |
| Overnight | Deep blue with warm lamp glow |

**Fixed colors with meaning.** VU amber is always you, rival teal is always them, on-air red is danger only. These must read inside every wash, so each daypart gets a grayscale test. Color never carries meaning alone, because glowing amber and glowing teal both turn near-white in grayscale and look the same to many color-blind players. Every you-versus-rival signal pairs color with three backups: shape (you are a round dot, the rival is a diamond), position (you are always on the left, the rival always on the right), and motion (your lights glow steady, the rival's blink). The rival's teal also glows a little dimmer and deeper than your amber, so the two read as different grays. Inside the booth the base materials stay warm: walnut, tobacco, aged cream. Outside the window, a city-pop dusk sky over the town.

**The tuner is the signature UI.** A big analog dial across the top of the desk view with your frequency and the rival's glowing on the band. The needle drifts toward whoever is winning the hour. It doubles as the main menu and the Steam capsule image.

**Built for our tools.** Every prop maps its faces to one shared palette texture, so everything Tripo makes matches automatically and a recolor is a single image edit. Animation is mechanical only: platters, faders, needles, the tuner. Townsfolk on the map are simple, chunky figures with idle loops at most.

**Music is the biggest open risk.** A radio game lives on its songs, and licensed tracks are off the table. We need short original loops for each format, from royalty-free libraries, commissioned work, or AI music tools with clean commercial licenses. DJ chatter is on-screen text with a radio filter, not voice acting. This gets decided before production starts.

## v1 scope

Starting targets, to be tuned once the first playable is fun:

| Content | v1 target |
| --- | --- |
| Formats | 3 (Classic Rock, Hip-Hop Heat, Country Roads) |
| Cards | About 30 per format plus 30 shared, so roughly 120 |
| Gear and staff | About 30 |
| Rivals | 12 regular, 4 Prime Time elites |
| Bosses | 3 (one per book) |
| Station Events | About 20 |
| Markets | 6 difficulty tiers |

**Out of scope for v1:** multiplayer, voice acting, licensed music, character animation, the Talk Radio format, an explorable 3D world, and a story campaign.

**Steam setup:** Unity 6 (URP), published under Consumed Media. Steam Deck verified, full controller support, achievements, cloud saves. A Coming Soon page goes up early to collect wishlists, with a demo for Steam Next Fest. Price target is $14.99 to $19.99, the band where genre games did best in the research.

## Open questions

- **Music:** where the format loops come from, and the budget for it.
- **Art style:** direction is set (Kind Words 2 spirit, desk view for fights, dioramas elsewhere). Next, prove it with one greybox booth and one diorama test scene before any bulk Tripo work.
- **Fight length:** does a daypart run a fixed number of turns, or until one side's audience breaks?
- **Subtitle:** something like Turn the Dial: A Radio Roguelike, for Steam search.
- **Trademark:** run a search on the name in the video game class before spending on branding.
